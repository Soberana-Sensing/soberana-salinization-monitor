[4]. Generally, these changes are interpreted negatively as signs of degradation, as shown in Richards [8] book entitled The degradation of landscapes in West Africa. Michel [9] describes in Casamance the increase in bush res, slash- and-burn cultivation, and logging which in addition to the destruction of resources by re, have transformed the ora.
This degradation is often attributed to the long period of reduced rainfall [1012] and to demographic, social, eco- nomic, and political transformations [5, 1316]. The two factors are often considered together [6, 17].
However, since the late 1990s, there has been a rever- sal of certain environmental trends in rainfall and vegeta- tion. Indeed, there has been a recovery in rainfall [1821], which, according to the scientic literature, has resulted in a greening of the Sahel [2, 2228]. Charney's [29] predictive model envisaged the signicant decrease in rainfall in West Africa as being linked to the signicant increase in mean albedo, which in turn depended on the vegetation cover of the land. As the latest observations were not consistent with this model at the Sahel scale, new avenues have since been explored concerning the links between climate and vegetation [30, 31]. As was the case in the article by [4], which mentioned that the regreening dynamics of West Africa have been repeatedly observed in Sahelian latitudes and have been explained by the return to wetter conditions. However, although various studies have highlighted the link between changes in plant cover and uctuations in rainfall over time and space [32], the consequences of this variability in rainfall on qualitative (species and quantitative (density, biomass changes in vegetation are still relatively unknown. Several regional or comparative studies have demonstrated a signicant response of NDVI to interannual rainfall variations in African regions such as the Sahel [24, 33, 34], East Africa [35, 36], the Kalahari region [37, 38] or Southern Africa as a whole [39, 40]. More spatially limited studies are available for countries such as Ethiopia [41], Sudan [42], Côte d'Ivoire and Benin [4, 43], Senegal [4446] or parts of South Africa [47], among others. In some regions, high spatial variability of NDVI sensitivity to inter-annual rain- fall variations has been found [40, 48, 49]. The Sudanian and Guinean zones are little studied and this paper is justi- ed as it complements the bibliography focused on bio- climates with a short and marked rainy and green season, with a study at the boundary between these bioclimates and the evergreen ones. This requires a general re-exam- ination of rainfall-plant activity relationships, which are less well known than for the Sahel. To this end, the trends in the vegetative activity are mapped and explanations are searched in the relationship with rainfall. The questions that this study aims to answer are the following: What are the rhythms of plant activity in Casamance? Has vegeta-
tion activity in Casamance as a whole uctuated over the last 30years? What is the relationship between vegeta- tive activity and rain? Is the recovery of rainfall since the 1990s (after the drought from 1969) reected in the trends of the NDVI since 1982?
2 Study Scales andData 2.1 Study Sites The Casamance region is located in the south of Senegal and is bordered to the north by the Gambia, to the south by Bissau Guinea and the Republic of Guinea, to the east by the Anambé basin (Kolda) and the Niokolo-Koba national park and to the west by the Atlantic Ocean (Fig.1). It com- prises the administrative regions of Ziguinchor, Sedhiou, and Kolda. Only the rst two, also called Lower and Middle Casamance, are studied here. Casamance occupies an area of 28,340 km2 [13, 50, 51]. This geographical situation gives it, within the Sahelian countries, particular characteristics with a Sudano-Guinean type climate, forest vegetation, and a relatively dense hydrographic network [5255]. Accord- ing to the latest general census of population, housing, agri- culture, and livestock [56], Casamance has a population of 1,886,638 inhabitants, distributed as follows for the studied regions: Ziguinchor (621,171) and Sedhiou (517,016). The climate of the region is Sudano-Guinean, with a rainy season from June to October, with maximum intensity in August and September, and a dry season from November to May [20]. A zonal climatic gradient contrasts the north with the short rainy season (2 to 3months) and low rainfall (500 to 800mm) and the south with the long rainy season (3 to 4months) and high rainfall (13001600mm) [20]. The vegetation cover is largely dominated by woody formations, diversied according to latitude, topography, and human activities. The vegetation of Casamance is characterized by a distinction between mangrove vegetation and dryland vegetation. Mangrove vegetation varies spatially on a very large scale in height and density depending on the height and frequency of ooding by the tide and the water salinity. It is an evergreen vegetation whose chlorophyll activity varies little during the year [57]. Its regeneration since the rainfall recovery has already been demonstrated [58]. The dryland vegetation, studied in this paper, varies spatially along the climatic gradient from low, open, deciduous Sudano-Sahe- lian vegetation to high, dense, evergreen Sudano-Guinean vegetation. The low spatial resolution images used for this study are NDVI (Normalized Dierence Vegetation Index) images. NDVI values range from  1 to + 1, with negative values corresponding to surfaces other than vegetation cover (e.g. water, clouds), for which the red reectance is higher than the NIR reectance. Vegetation formations have positive NDVI val- ues, generally between 0.1 and 0.5, with the highest values corresponding to the densest and most active cover. In both these datasets, the NDVI values are stretched from 0 to 8000 (NOAA) or 0 to 10,000 (MODIS).

3.2 Temporal Analysis: Kendall Correlation andDecadal Comparison ofNDVI Curves The statistical processing of the time series of NDVI- NOAA and MODIS data was done in two parts. The rst was to determine whether there was a trend in each time series constituted by each pixel of an image. The second part was to describe the changes in terms of mean annual patterns of vegetation activity between the two periods. The MannKendall trend test was used for the rst part. The
choice of a trend test depends on several criteria, including the sample size, the distribution of the analysis variable, and the nature of the expected trends [74]. The MannKen- dall test (with seasonality) was chosen for its proven robust- ness [7477] and because it is non-parametric (it makes no assumptions about the underlying distribution of the data).
The MannKendall tests are based on the calculation of Kendall's tau measuring the association between the meas- ured value (NDVI) and the rank of the observation in the time series.
The calculation is associated with signicance tests where the probability (p) represents the measure of the need to reject a null hypothesis. Signicance maps will not be pre- sented, but only signicant trends are apparent on the map and graphs. In addition, it is important to note that all strong correlations (negative or positive in the published maps (Figs.7, 8, 9, 10 and 11)) are signicant at the P < 0.1 test.
The same treatments were performed on both time series (MODIS and NOAA).
Then, the NOAA NDVI series was split into two periods on either side of the 1997 cut-o date separating the dry period from the current normal period. The mean curves (for these two periods) of the classes were then plotted in a graph showing which seasonal change was detected by the Kendall correlation.

Nevertheless, the relationship between plant activity and rainfall needs to be examined more closely.
The relationship between rainfall and chlorophyll activ- ity shows that, on average, over the last 18years, the rainy season began in May with a maximum in July, a decrease in August, fairly average rainfall in September, and almost no rainfall between October and November (Fig.7).
In the dryland vegetation around Ziguinchor, the chloro- phyll activity season begins in June. The plant activity curve follows the rainfall curve at the beginning of the season, with a one-month lag. However, the activity, always increas- ing with a peak in August, a NDVI strong in September and October, slows down little by little from November and the end of December, which gives the image of vegetation which nishes its foliar development and gradually reaches the optimum of its season of activity. It, therefore, appears that the vegetations reactions to rainfall are quite strong, but that they show a time lag with the rains. Indeed, there is a link between the two, but it is not synchronous. The vegeta- tion reaction starts a few weeks after the arrival of the rst rains and the peak in vegetative activity is several weeks after the end of the rainy season. The vegetations reaction to rainfall, therefore, shows inertia.
To prove that the link is not obvious, the scatter plot (Fig.8) shows that there is no relationship between rainfall and NDVI (r2 = 0.17). Contrary to dry areas like the Sahel, we do not have a clear and synchronous relationship between rainfall and NDVI in Casamance.
The interannual variation of NDVI amplitude and annual rainfall (Fig.9) shows a rst period (20002008) where the relationship is unclear, followed by a clearer relationship after 2008. However, the correlation remains low (Table1).
The set of correlations (simple, lagged, and cumulative) is shown in Table1 following Richard & Poccard, [40] methodology. This conrms that there is no direct relation- ship between NDVI and rainfall (no correlation between the values of NDVI and rainfall at the same period). However, there are strong relationships that appear in both the cumu- lative (correlation between the NDVI at one period and a longer previous period) and lagged correlations (correlation between NDVI at a period and rainfall at a previous period).
The lagged correlations are higher than the simple ones. The correlations increase from the 16-day lag to the 144-day lag and then decrease. The correlations with rainfall totals are higher than the simple ones. The correlations increase from the 32-day to the 64-day lag and then decrease. The

5 Discussion The NDVI time series were analyzed with the aim of map- ping and explaining the patterns of their relationship with
annual rainfall variations. Large spatial dierences in the response of photosynthetic activity to rainfall are observed in Africa [43, 49]. The data used for this kind of study on a regional scale are those of the NOAA and MODIS, which can provide bi-monthly summaries for the whole of the Sahel from the 1980s. These data conrm that climate is an important factor explaining NDVI variations in Casamance.
They allow the study of the relationship between climate and vegetation. For changes not related to climate, they allow to detect anthropic drivers. Kendall correlations, at the regional scale, show that measurable changes are a function of the observation window at both spatial and temporal levels [79].
Cartographic perception either accentuates or attenuates the current trends depending on these two variables. We can therefore speak of a perception bias linked to the upstream choice of the study area and the temporality of the phenom- enon studied. The same treatment was carried out on similar
data. With the NDVI-MODIS images, the time series only covers the last 20years which does not show a major climatic trend, as the series begins after the last major mile- stone of 1998 used to mark the return to normal rainfall con- ditions [20, 78]. With the NDVI-NOAA images, the period is long enough to see the major climatic trends over the last 35years and the series is fairly well centered around this pivotal date of 1998. Between 1982 (one of the driest peri- ods of the century) and 2015 (almost 20years after the rains resumed), the trends observed show signicant increases in NDVI after these droughts for 72.5% of the pixels accord- ing to the MannKendall test. The length of the time series and the starting date strongly inuence these treatments [64, 79]. Furthermore, the analysis of the MannKendall map on the MODIS NDVI shows localized changes in particular agricultural areas (situated on the banks of the Casamance River in the Sedhiou region). These changes are therefore most likely to be explained by changes in land use and agriculture (fallows, cashew orchard development) than in rainfall. However, for the NDVI-NOAA, it is impossible to show such changes with 8km pixels. The impossibility of detecting local changes in land cover perhaps favors the identication of climatic trends. The eect of time bounds and pixel sizes converge to highlight recent changes in land cover for MODIS and 40-year changes in climate for NOAA.
Changing the spatial or temporal scale reveals either human activity or climate change with the same data processing (NDVI-NOAA and NDVI-MODIS) as already observed by Andrieu [4] over the Ivory Coast.
Concerning the characterization of these changes, on the one hand, the increase in NDVI for the whole region according to NOAA is contrary to the claims of irreversible 'desertication generalized in the Sahel [80]. [8, 10, 81, 82]. Recent results based on NDVI-MODIS (20002019) and NOAA (19822015) time series across the MannKen- dall show an increase in vegetation indices over large areas in Casamance since the mid-1990s. This is consistent with increased rainfall in the Sahel and has been interpreted as a recovery of vegetation [83] in response to increased rainfall [21]. This discourse is reinforced by several remote sensing studies at the regional scale that conrm the thesis of a Sahelian regreening as a direct consequence of the resumption of rainfall from the 1990s onwards [2, 22, 24, 27, 64, 79, 84]. These papers are mainly based on the analysis of inter-annual NDVI trends. The maps included in these articles have contributed to the construction of a collective vision of regreening in the Sahelian region, which our study wishes to complete with a study of the southwestern fringe of West Africa. It is important to qualify this statement as the greening trend in Casamance is not uniform, with only a few cross-out locations showing signicant negative NDVI
trends (1.6% for MODIS and 0.3% for NOAA), which are due to the clearing of natural vegetation for annual crops, bushres and logging in the Sedhiou and Ziguinchor regions.
Observations of such phenomena (decline of vegetation in certain areas) can help to reconcile the theories of regreen- ing and degradation. For this reason, several authors [30, 8588] mention the importance of integrating the notion of the social-ecological system, which also allows for a bet- ter appreciation of human society/biophysical environment interactions at dierent scales than other concepts such as the geosystem or the ecocomplexan approach that is rel- evant in a Sahelian context where the complex of perma- nent changes is marked by biophysical, socio-economic and political mutations.
6 Conclusion Over the past four decades, vegetation has shown a regional response to improved rainfall in the form of increased inten- sity of vegetation activity after the rainy season. 72.5% of the pixels show a signicant positive trend on NOAA NDVI trend analyses indicating a general increase in the vegeta- tion index, which was interpreted as a greening of the region that ts with the rainfall recovery as 1982 is the dri- est period. The search for relationships between rainfall and chlorophyll activity showed a strong link with a correlation lagged by 144days (r2 = 0.86) or cumulated over 2months (r2 = 0.76). This result opens the way to further examinations or even re-examinations of the nature of the links between the functioning of the vegetation and its physiognomy.
Examining the recent period for the Casamance alone with the same methods at a ner resolution, there is apparent overall stability as well as a local patches of positive and negative trends. They both correspond with known land-use changes in terms of deforestation or fallows and new cashew orchards. This study, therefore, conrms that Casamance is not in a process of strong degradation, at least not at this scale with these observation angles. However, when com- pared to older periods or when focusing on some precise species heavily exploited (e.g., Pterocarpus erinaceus), the degradation appears [80]. From a methodological point of view, it conrms that these results are very dependent on temporal and spatial scales, which does not question their robustness, provided that the relevance of the choice of scale and resolution is tested according to the question posed. This also encourages multiple treatments (multiscale, multitem- poral, multisensor).

Contrary to claims of widespread irreversible degradation of vegetation and landscapes in West Africa, a recent increase
in seasonal vegetation indices of Sahelian areas has been observed, which has been interpreted as a consequence of the
rainfall recovery after the major droughts (19681994). This paper aims at revealing the climatic drivers of such greening
in Casamance. A multi-scalar and multi-satellite remote sensing approach was implemented to study the temporal trends of
vegetation activity and their relationships with rainfall in Lower and middle Casamance. The datasets used are the NOAA NDVI (GIMMS) from 1982 to 2015, the MODIS NDVI (MOD13Q1) from 2000 to 2019, and rainfall data from the Ziguin-
chor station from 1982 to 2017. These two NDVI time series were studied, on the one hand, with a classication method to
discretize the dierent vegetations according to the rhythms and intensities of their vegetation activity throughout the year
and, on the other hand, with MannKendalls correlation to reveal the trends. Almost three-quarters (72.5%) of the pixels
show a signicant positive trend (regreening) between 1982 and 2015. The simple correlation between NDVI and rainfall is very low (r2 = 0.17) but both lagged correlation (r2 = 0.86) and the correlation between NDVI and cumulated rainfall of longer periods (r2
= 0.75) are strong. In other terms, after 1998 stronger rainfall in July and August give stronger NDVI in
October and November. While a rainfall positive trend since the 1980s appears to be the main causal factor for the increase
in vegetation indices, negative trends were also locally observed that are not explained by the rainfall-vegetation relationship and thus hypothetically a human-induced change.
